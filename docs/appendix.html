Appendix. Many trials

What many trials do to an average. Examinable. What comes after it is not.

What happens to an average over many trials is examinable. What comes after it is not: sampling error, confidence intervals and hypothesis testing are for the second half of the course, and no midterm question needs them.

Section A.1Many trials

One throw of a fair die gives a whole number from 1 to 6, and the six values are equally likely. Their average, 1 plus 2 plus 3 plus 4 plus 5 plus 6 divided by 6, is 3.5, a value the die itself can never show.

Now throw the die several times and take the average of those throws. Do that again and again, and look at how far apart the highest and lowest averages are. The table was started in class with the first row and left for the simulator to finish. Below is one run of it.

Each row is the highest and lowest of a thousand averages, so these are the extremes that turned up, not the extremes that could. Ten throws averaging 1.00 is possible and simply did not occur in a thousand tries. The fewer throws in each average, the more your own run will differ from this one.

Two things change together as the number of throws goes up. The averages lie closer to 3.5, so the difference between the highest and the lowest falls from 5.00 to 0.32. At the same time the averages themselves get finer. With ten throws an average is a multiple of one tenth, with a hundred throws a multiple of one hundredth, so the possible values are closer and closer together.

That second change is the same idea as 2.6, seen from the other side. A single throw is recorded on a coarse scale with six values on it. Averaging many throws of the same coarse thing produces a quantity that behaves as though it were continuous. Recording decides how fine a single value is, and averaging decides how fine a summary of many values is.

The class ended on what that means away from dice. Over a short run a person can be lucky or unlucky, and the luck is most of what you see. Over many trials the good and the bad luck cancel, and what is left is the thing you were trying to measure. Reading one quarter, one cohort or one trainer's year is reading a short run.

The question to ask

If I repeated this whole exercise tomorrow, how different would my answer be?
